## Abstract

This technical paper begins by introducing the concept of aquaponics, including a brief history of its development and its place within the larger category of soil-less culture and modern agriculture. It discusses the main theoretical concepts of aquaponics, including the nitrogen cycle and the nitrification process, the role of bacteria, and the concept of balancing an aquaponic unit. It then moves on to cover important considerations of water quality parameters, water testing, and water sourcing for aquaponics, as well as methods and theories of unit design, including the three main methods of aquaponic systems: media beds, nutrient film technique, and deep water culture.

The publication discusses in detail the three groups of living organisms (bacteria, plants and fish) that make up the aquaponic ecosystem. It also presents management strategies and troubleshooting practices, as well as related topics, specifically highlighting local and sustainable sources of aquaponic inputs.

The publication also includes nine appendixes that present other key topics: ideal conditions for common plants grown in aquaponics; chemical and biological controls of common pests and diseases including a compatible planting guide; common fish diseases and related symptoms, causes and remedies; tools to calculate the ammonia produced and biofiltration media required for a certain fish stocking density and amount of fish feed added; production of homemade fish feed; guidelines and considerations for establishing aquaponic units; a cost–benefit analysis of a small-scale, media bed aquaponic unit; a comprehensive guide to building small-scale versions of each of the three aquaponic methods; and a brief summary of this publication designed as a supplemental handout for outreach, extension and education.

## Summary

This is FAO Fisheries and Aquaculture Technical Paper No. 589, a 262-page practitioner manual written by five FAO consultants/staff as a general-audience bridge between aquaculture and hydroponics practitioners who each previously knew only their own half of aquaponics. Rather than reporting a new study, it synthesises established recirculating-aquaculture and hydroponics engineering knowledge, aquaponics-specific research from named specialists (James Rakocy/UVI, Wilson Lennard, Nick Savidov, Mohammad Abdus Salam), and the authors' hands-on practitioner experience into prescriptive guidance covering: what aquaponics is, its history, and where it is and is not appropriate (Ch. 1); the nitrogen cycle, nitrifying bacteria, and the "feed rate ratio" (grams of daily fish feed per m² of growing space) used to balance a unit (Ch. 2); the five key water-quality parameters — dissolved oxygen, pH, temperature, total nitrogen (ammonia/nitrite/nitrate), and hardness — with stated tolerance ranges for fish, plants and bacteria plus a "compromise" range for running all three together (Ch. 3); construction of media bed, nutrient film technique (NFT) and deep water culture (DWC) systems (Ch. 4, sampled only); bacterial biofiltration and system cycling (Ch. 5, sampled only); plant biology, nutrient deficiencies, and pest/disease management specific to a system where synthetic chemical pesticides cannot be used (Ch. 6); fish anatomy, feed conversion ratio, water-quality tolerances and species profiles for seven commercial species (Ch. 7); a worked numerical example of the feed-rate-ratio design calculation, fish-stocking-density and biofilter-sizing rules of thumb, staggered-stocking tables, and a troubleshooting table (Ch. 8); and supplementary topics including organic fertilizer alternatives, alternative fish feeds, and small-scale case studies (Ch. 9, sampled only). The nine appendixes add a 12-plant growing-condition reference table, pest/disease control tables, a step-by-step ammonia/biofilter-media sizing calculation, a homemade-fish-feed guide, a pre-setup checklist, a cost–benefit worked example for a domestic-scale unit, and full construction instructions for all three system types. Throughout, the document presents ranges and rules of thumb as prescriptive guidance rather than as data from a controlled study of its own; the closest things to "results" in the document are worked arithmetic examples (the feed-rate-ratio calculation, the ammonia/biofilter sizing calculation, and the cost–benefit tables) built from the stated ranges and assumptions, not from measured trial data.

## The nitrogen cycle and balancing an aquaponic unit (Ch. 2)

**This document's account:** Fish waste (mostly ammonia, NH₃) is converted by two groups of nitrifying bacteria — ammonia-oxidizing bacteria (AOB, e.g. *Nitrosomonas* spp., optimal pH 7.2–7.8) and nitrite-oxidizing bacteria (NOB, e.g. *Nitrobacter* spp., optimal pH 7.2–8.2) — first to nitrite then to nitrate, the form plants use most readily (Section 2.1.1–2.2, Table 2.1, p. 11–16). Bacterial tolerance ranges are stated as 17–34 °C, pH 6–8.5, ammonia <3 mg/L, nitrite <3 mg/L, nitrate <400 mg/L, DO 4–8 mg/L (Table 2.1); below 17 °C bacterial productivity decreases, and below 10 °C productivity "can be reduced by 50 percent or more" (Section 2.3.3, p. 15). The **feed rate ratio** — grams of daily fish feed per m² of plant growing area — is presented as the single simplified balancing metric, assuming an industry-standard 32-percent-protein feed: 40–50 g/m²/day for leafy greens, 50–80 g/m²/day for fruiting vegetables (Box 2, p. 17–18; restated Ch. 8). Nitrogen-testing thresholds for rebalancing: if ammonia or nitrite exceed 1 mg/L, biofiltration is inadequate; if nitrate stays below 10 mg/L for several weeks, feed rate can be cautiously increased; if nitrate exceeds 150 mg/L for several weeks, partial water exchange is recommended (Section 2.4.4, p. 18–19).

## The five water-quality parameters (Ch. 3)

**This document's account:** Table 3.1 gives separate tolerance ranges for warm-water fish, cold-water fish, plants and bacteria across temperature, pH, ammonia, nitrite, nitrate and DO; Table 3.2 gives the "ideal compromise" for running all three organism groups together: temperature 18–30 °C, pH 6–7, ammonia <1 mg/L, nitrite <1 mg/L, nitrate 5–150 mg/L, DO >5 mg/L (p. 22). Individual parameter detail: optimum DO for all three organisms is 5–8 mg/L, with some fish (carp, tilapia) tolerating as low as 2–3 mg/L (Section 3.2.1); pH 6.0–6.5 gives full nutrient availability to plants, pH 7.5 can cause iron/phosphorus/manganese "nutrient lock-out," and fish tolerate pH swings poorly (>0.3 pH-unit change within 12–24 hours is flagged as problematic in Ch. 7, Section 7.3.2, p. 108) (Section 3.2.2); water hardness optimum for aquaponics is stated as "about 60–140 mg/litre" for both general hardness (GH) and carbonate hardness/alkalinity (KH) (Section 3.2.5, p. 27), with a separate hardness-classification scale given as soft 0–60, moderately hard 60–120, hard 120–180, very hard >180 mg/L. Salinity is flagged as generally too high above 1 500 μS/cm conductivity or 800 ppm TDS (Section 3.4, p. 29–30).

⚠️MINOR — **Two overlapping-but-not-identical "hardness optimum" statements, both from this document, not from different sources:** Section 3.2.5's summary states the optimum for "both hardness types" is "about 60–140 mg/litre" (p. 27), which sits inside, but does not exactly match the boundary of, the separately-given hardness classification scale where "moderately hard" is defined as 60–120 mg/L and "hard" as 120–180 mg/L (same section, p. 28). These are not contradictory data points — one is a recommended target range and the other is a general hardness-naming convention — but a reader skimming only one of the two could reasonably expect them to align exactly at 140 mg/L, which the classification scale does not name as a boundary. ## Fish species profiles and feed conversion (Ch. 7)

**This document's account:** Table 7.1 (p. 109) gives vital/optimal temperature, TAN, nitrite, DO, feed-protein percentage and stated grow-out growth rate for seven commercial species: common carp (600 g in 9–11 months), Nile tilapia (600 g in 6–8 months), channel catfish (400 g in 9–10 months), rainbow trout (1 000 g in 14–16 months), flathead mullet (750 g in 9–11 months), giant river prawn (30 g in 4–5 months), and barramundi (400 g in 9–10 months). Tilapia FCR is stated as 1.4–1.8 "in good conditions" (Section 7.2.3, p. 107) — i.e. 1.4–1.8 kg feed per kg of tilapia growth; this is presented as a general industry figure, not a value this document measured. Maximum recommended fish stocking density is 20 kg fish per 1 000 litres of water (10–20 kg/1 000 L "for the small-scale units described in this publication"), reduced to half that (1 kg/100 L) for tanks smaller than 500 litres (Section 8.1.2, p. 125). Catfish (Clariidae) can be stocked much more densely — "up to 150 kg/m³" — because of comprehensive additional mechanical filtration and their air-breathing physiology (Section 7.4.3, p. 113).

## Plant nutrient requirements and 12-plant growing guide (Ch. 6, Appendix 1)

**This document's account:** Plants need six macronutrients (N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) and six-plus micronutrients (Fe, Mn, B, Zn, Cu, Mo); the three most commonly deficient in a fish-feed-only nutrient supply are iron, potassium and calcium, because "fish simply do not need the same amounts of iron, potassium and calcium that plants require" (Section 6.2.4, p. 89). Recommended supplementation: chelated iron to reach roughly 2 mg/L in the system generally, or 5 mL of chelated iron per m² of grow bed when deficiency is suspected (Section 6.2.3, p. 89), and 1–2 mg/L for the first three months of a new unit specifically (Section 8.3.1, p. 128). Appendix 1 (p. 169–182) gives pH, spacing, germination temperature/time, growth time, optimal temperature, light exposure, mature plant size and recommended aquaponic method for 12 named vegetables/herbs (basil, cauliflower, lettuce, cucumbers, eggplant, peppers, tomato, beans and peas, head cabbage, broccoli, Swiss chard, parsley), each with a prose growing-conditions/growing-instructions/harvesting section. These are presented as the manual's consolidated practical guidance drawn from "long-standing aquaponic farming, ... horticulture manuals ... extension papers, and the professional experience of farmers and researchers" (p. 169), not as data from a controlled trial reported in this document.

## Worked design calculations (Ch. 8, Appendix 4, Appendix 7)

**This document's account — feed rate ratio worked example (Section 8.1.1, p. 123–124):** to produce 25 heads of lettuce/week (100 heads over a 4-week grow cycle) at 25 heads/m², a system needs 4 m² of growing space; at 50 g feed/m²/day for leafy greens, that requires 200 g feed/day; at fish eating 1–2 percent of body weight/day, that requires 10–20 kg of fish biomass. This is arithmetic built from the manual's own stated ranges, not a measured outcome.

**Ammonia/biofilter sizing worked example (Appendix 4, p. 191–192):** of the nitrogen in fish feed, the manual states ~30 percent is retained in fish body mass and ~70 percent is lost — 15 percent as undigested solid waste (of which ~60 percent is removed by clarifiers, leaving ~6 percent to degrade into ammonia in the water) and 55 percent excreted directly as ammonia or ammonia-precursors, for a combined ~61 percent of feed nitrogen becoming ammonia subject to nitrification. Worked example: 200 g of 32-percent-protein feed (nitrogen assumed at 16 percent of protein) yields ~7.5 g ammonia/day, requiring ~13.3 m² of nitrifying-bacteria surface area at a conservative removal rate of 0.57 g ammonia/m²/day, which in turn requires ~44.3 litres of volcanic tuff (SSA 300 m²/m³) — i.e. "1 litre of tuff can convert the ammonia obtained by 4.5 g of feed." Table A4.1 tabulates this feed-to-media ratio for nine biofilter media types (coarse sand through coarse gravel), spanning specific surface areas from 150 to 5 000 m²/m³.

**Cost–benefit worked example (Appendix 7, p. 205–207), explicitly a domestic-scale illustrative model, not a measured farm's accounts:** for a 1 000-litre-fish-tank / 3 m²-growing-space media bed unit growing 50 percent lettuce and 50 percent tomatoes, stated capital cost is USD 700 (Table A7.1), monthly operating cost USD 26.45 (Table A7.2, itemized: seedlings, fingerlings, electricity, water, fish feed, miscellaneous), annual revenue USD 758.40 from an assumed 360 lettuce heads + 54 kg tomatoes + 30 kg fish at stated local (Israeli) market prices accessed 17 September 2013 (Table A7.3), giving an annual net profit of USD 441 and a stated payback period of 19 months (Table A7.4). The manual is explicit that "the figures given in the tables are only intended as guidelines for new users" and that yields, prices and costs "may influence" the outcome considerably (p. 205) — this is presented throughout as a worked illustrative model built on stated assumptions, not as measured accounts from an actual operating unit.
